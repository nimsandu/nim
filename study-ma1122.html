01 · Differentiation and applications · 1.1

Gradient, tangent lines and rates of change

Lecture 01 · pp. 1–5Reference · Side A §1

Core idea

The gradient describes steepness. For a graph, it is the slope of a line. In a changing system, it describes how quickly one quantity changes compared with another. The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change and the slope of the tangent line. In single-variable calculus the gradient is a number; in multivariable calculus it is a vector.

Average rate of change

f(b)−f(a)b−a

Change in output divided by change in input. It is the slope of the secant line.

Instantaneous rate of change

f′(a)=limh→0f(a+h)−f(a)h

Limit of the average rate as the input change approaches zero.

Tangent and normal lines

At P(a, f(a)), the tangent slope is mt = f′(a). The normal line is perpendicular to the tangent.

y−f(a)=f′(a)(x−a)
Tangent equation · normal slope = −1/f′(a), provided f′(a) ≠ 0.
  • If the tangent is horizontal, the normal is vertical: x = a.
  • Parallel lines have equal slopes.
  • Perpendicular non-vertical lines have slopes whose product is −1.
  • Rate units are output units per input unit.
Method · tangent or normal
  1. Identify the point (a, f(a)).
  2. Differentiate f and evaluate f′(a).
  3. For a tangent, use slope f′(a). For a normal, use −1/f′(a), unless the tangent is horizontal.
  4. Substitute the point and slope into point-slope form.
Worked example · lecture-style

Find the tangent to f(x)=x² at (1,1).

1. Differentiate: f′(x)=2x.
2. At x=1, the tangent slope is f′(1)=2.
3. Point-slope form: y−1=2(x−1), so y=2x−1.
Worked example · normal line

For y=x√x at (1,1), write y=x3/2. Then y′=(3/2)√x, so the tangent slope at x=1 is 3/2. The normal slope is −2/3.

Normal: y−1=−(2/3)(x−1).
02 · Differentiation and applications · 1.2.1–1.2.2

The derivative as a function

Lecture 01 · pp. 6–8Reference · Side A §1

Two forms of the definition

At one fixed input a, the derivative is a number. Letting the input vary gives the derivative function f′(x), which gives the tangent slope at every point where it exists. For a change from x₁ to x₂, Δx=x₂−x₁ and Δy=f(x₂)−f(x₁); the difference quotient Δy/Δx is the average rate of change.

f′(a)=limh→0f(a+h)−f(a)hf′(x)=limh→0f(x+h)−f(x)h

Derivative notation

For y=f(x), the lecture uses the equivalent notations:

f′(x)=y′=dydx=dfdx=ddxf(x)

D and d/dx are differentiation operators. The value at x=a can be written f′(a) or (dy/dx)|x=a.

Method · first principles
  1. Write f(x+h).
  2. Form [f(x+h)−f(x)]/h.
  3. Expand and simplify; factor or rationalize if needed.
  4. Cancel h only after noting h≠0.
  5. Take the limit as h→0.
Worked example · lecture p. 4

Find the derivative of f(x)=x²−8x+9 at x=a.

f(a+h)−f(a) = (a+h)²−8(a+h)+9−(a²−8a+9).
Divide by h and simplify: (2ah+h²−8h)/h = 2a+h−8.
Take h→0: f′(a)=2a−8.
First-principles derivation of two rules · lecture p. 8For a constant f(x)=c, the difference quotient is (c−c)/h=0, so f′(x)=0. For g(x)=c f(x), the quotient becomes c[f(x+h)−f(x)]/h; taking the limit gives g′(x)=c f′(x).
03 · Differentiation and applications · 1.2.3–1.2.5

Differentiability, continuity and where derivatives fail

Lecture 01 · pp. 7–9Reference · Side A §1

Definitions and theorem

A function is differentiable at a if f′(a) exists as a finite number. It is differentiable on an interval if it is differentiable at every point in that interval.

TheoremIf f is differentiable at a, then f is continuous at a. The converse is false: continuity does not guarantee differentiability.

Three common failure cases in the lecture

  • Corner or cusp: left- and right-hand slopes do not agree.
  • Discontinuity: the function is not continuous at the point.
  • Vertical tangent: the tangent slope becomes unbounded, so no finite derivative exists.
Method · test differentiability at a point
  1. Write the difference quotient.
  2. Find its left-hand and right-hand limits separately.
  3. If both are finite and equal, the derivative exists. If they differ or either is not finite, it does not.
Worked example · f(x)=|x| at x=0
For h>0, |h|/h=1, so the right-hand limit is 1.
For h<0, |h|/h=−1, so the left-hand limit is −1.
The one-sided limits differ. Therefore f is continuous but not differentiable at 0.

05 · Differentiation and applications · 1.3.6–1.3.8

Exponential, product and quotient rules

Lecture 01 · pp. 11–12Reference · Side A §2

Natural exponential

ddxex=ex

Product rule

(uv)′=u′v+uv′

Use when two functions are multiplied. Differentiate the first and keep the second, then keep the first and differentiate the second; add the results.

Worked example

Differentiate y=x²(3x+2).

Let u=x² and v=3x+2. Then u′=2x and v′=3.
y′=(2x)(3x+2)+x²(3).
y′=9x²+4x.

Quotient rule

(uv)′=vu′−uv′v2,v≠0

Use when one function is divided by another. The denominator must be nonzero wherever the quotient is defined.

Worked example

Differentiate y=(3x+1)/(x²+2).

Numerator u=3x+1, u′=3; denominator v=x²+2, v′=2x.
y′=[(x²+2)(3)−(3x+1)(2x)]/(x²+2)².
y′=(−3x²−2x+6)/(x²+2)².

Lecture 01 · pp. 13–14Reference · Side A §2

Chain rule

Use when one function is inside another. Differentiate the outside function while leaving the inside expression in place, then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

F(x)=f(g(x))⇒F′(x)=f′(g(x))g′(x)dydx=dydududx

Power rule combined with chain rule

ddx[u(x)]n=n[u(x)]n−1u′(x)

Differentiate F(x)=√(x²+1).

Exponential derivatives

Lecture 01 · pp. 11, 14Reference · Side A §2

The natural exponential function has base e. A general exponential function has a fixed positive base a.

ddxeu=euu′ddxau=aulnau′

a is a fixed base with a>0; u=u(x) is the exponent; u′ is the derivative of the exponent; e is Euler’s number.

Worked example

Differentiate y=3²ˣ.

Base a=3 and exponent u=2x, so u′=2.
Apply the general exponential rule: y′=3²ˣ ln(3)·2.
y′=2·3²ˣ ln 3.
09 · Differentiation and applications · 1.3.13

Implicit differentiation

Lecture 01 · pp. 14–15Reference · Side A §5

Some relationships between x and y are not written as y=f(x). Differentiate both sides with respect to x, treating y as a function of x. Every time a y-expression is differentiated, use the chain rule and include y′=dy/dx.

Method
  1. Differentiate the left and right sides with respect to x.
  2. Whenever y appears, differentiate it as a function of x (for example, d(y²)/dx=2yy′).
  3. Collect all terms containing y′ on one side.
  4. Factor y′ and divide to isolate dy/dx.
Worked example · lecture p. 15

Find dy/dx if x²+y²+xy=25.

Differentiate: 2x+2yy′+xy′+y=0.
Collect y′ terms: (2y+x)y′=−(2x+y).
dy/dx=−(2x+y)/(x+2y).

12 · Differentiation and applications · 1.3.16

Logarithmic differentiation

Lecture 01 · p. 17Reference · Side A §5

Use this method when products, quotients or powers make direct differentiation complicated—especially when both the base and exponent depend on x.

Method
  1. Take natural logarithms of both sides.
  2. Use logarithm laws to turn products into sums, quotients into differences, and powers into factors.
  3. Differentiate implicitly with respect to x.
  4. Solve for y′, then replace y with the original expression.
Worked example · lecture example

Differentiate y=x√x, x>0.

Take logs: ln y=√x ln x.
Differentiate: y′/y=(ln x)/(2√x)+1/√x.
Multiply by y=x√x.
y′=x√x(ln x+2)/(2√x).
13 · Differentiation and applications · 1.4

Maximum and minimum values: EVT, Fermat and critical numbers

Lecture 01 · pp. 18–21Reference · Side A §6

Types of extrema

  • Absolute (global) maximum: greatest function value on the stated domain or interval.
  • Absolute (global) minimum: least function value.
  • Local maximum/minimum: greatest/least value compared with values sufficiently near the point.
  • The maximum and minimum values are called extreme values.
Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)If f is continuous on a closed interval [a,b], then f attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum at some points in [a,b]. The theorem guarantees existence; it does not locate the points.
Fermat’s TheoremIf f has a local maximum or minimum at c and f′(c) exists, then f′(c)=0.

A critical number is a number c in the domain of f where f′(c)=0 or f′(c) does not exist. A stationary number is a critical number where f′(c)=0.

Do not reverse Fermat’s theoremf′(c)=0 does not automatically mean there is a local maximum or minimum. For example, x³ has derivative zero at 0 but no extremum there.
Worked example · classify candidates

For f(x)=x³, f′(x)=3x², so x=0 is critical. But f increases on both sides of 0; therefore it is not a local maximum or minimum.

14 · Differentiation and applications · 1.4

The Closed Interval Method

Lecture 01 · pp. 21–22Reference · Side A §6

Use this to find absolute maximum and minimum values of a continuous function on a closed interval [a,b].

Three-step method
  1. Find the critical numbers of f in the open interval (a,b), then evaluate f at those numbers.
  2. Evaluate f at both endpoints a and b.
  3. Compare all values. The largest is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the absolute minimum.
Worked example · lecture p. 21

Find absolute extrema of f(x)=x³−3x²+1 on [−1/2,4].

f′(x)=3x²−6x=3x(x−2). Critical numbers: x=0 and x=2.
Evaluate: f(0)=1, f(2)=−3, f(−1/2)=1/8, f(4)=17.
Compare: absolute maximum 17 at x=4; absolute minimum −3 at x=2.
Small typo in the lecture solution: page 21 prints f(1/2)=1/8, but the interval endpoint is −1/2. The correct endpoint evaluation is f(−1/2)=1/8; the value itself is unchanged.
15 · Differentiation and applications · 1.5

Rolle’s Theorem and the Mean Value Theorem

Lecture 01 · pp. 22–24Reference · Side A §7

Rolle’s Theorem

Hypotheses: f is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and f(a)=f(b). Conclusion: there is at least one c in (a,b) such that f′(c)=0.

f(a)=f(b)⇒∃c∈(a,b):f′(c)=0

Mean Value Theorem (MVT)

Hypotheses: f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b). Then at least one c in (a,b) satisfies:

f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−a

Geometrically, the tangent slope at c equals the secant slope between the endpoints. In motion, at some time the instantaneous velocity equals the average velocity over the interval.

Method · theorem question
  1. Check every hypothesis explicitly.
  2. For Rolle, verify f(a)=f(b). For MVT, calculate the secant slope.
  3. Differentiate f.
  4. Set f′(c)=0 (Rolle) or f′(c)=(f(b)−f(a))/(b−a) (MVT).
  5. Solve and keep only c values in (a,b).
Worked example · MVT

Apply MVT to f(x)=x² on [1,3].

Polynomial: continuous on [1,3] and differentiable on (1,3), so the hypotheses hold.
Secant slope=(9−1)/(3−1)=4. Since f′(x)=2x, solve 2c=4.
c=2, which lies in (1,3).
Rolle’s Theorem application · unique root

The lecture asks you to prove that x³+x−1=0 has exactly one real root. To show there cannot be two distinct roots, suppose a<b are both roots. Then f(a)=f(b)=0. Rolle’s Theorem would force f′(c)=0 for some c between them. But f′(x)=3x²+1>0 for every real x, a contradiction. Therefore there is at most one real root. Since f(0)=−1 and f(1)=1 and f is continuous, a root exists between 0 and 1; hence there is exactly one.

The Rolle argument is the key uniqueness step. The existence step uses the sign change of the continuous function.

Consequences stated in the lecture

  • If f′(x)=0 throughout an interval, f is constant on that interval.
  • If f′(x)=g′(x) throughout an interval, then f−g is constant; equivalently, f(x)=g(x)+C.

Increasing, decreasing and local extreme values

Lecture 01 · pp. 24–26Reference · Side A §8
Increasing / Decreasing TestIf f′(x)>0 throughout an interval, f is increasing there. If f′(x)<0 throughout an interval, f is decreasing there.

Sign-chart method

  1. Find f′(x) and factor it if possible.
  2. Find points where f′=0 or is undefined, and include domain breaks.
  3. Use these points to split the domain into intervals.
  4. Determine the sign of f′ on each interval.
  5. Translate positive signs to increasing and negative signs to decreasing.

First Derivative Test

Sign change at critical number cConclusion
f′: + to −Local maximum at c
f′: − to +Local minimum at c
No sign changeNeither local maximum nor local minimum
Worked example · lecture p. 25–26

For f(x)=3x⁴−4x³−12x²+5:

f′(x)=12x(x−2)(x+1). Critical numbers: −1, 0, 2.
Sign of f′ across intervals: (−∞,−1): −; (−1,0): +; (0,2): −; (2,∞): +.
Therefore decreasing on (−∞,−1) and (0,2); increasing on (−1,0) and (2,∞).
At −1: − to +, local minimum f(−1)=0. At 0: + to −, local maximum f(0)=5. At 2: − to +, local minimum f(2)=−27.
17 · Differentiation and applications · 1.6

Concavity, inflection points and the Second Derivative Test

Lecture 01 · pp. 27–28Reference · Side A §8

Concavity describes the direction in which a graph bends. The lecture defines concave upward as the curve lying above its tangents, and concave downward as lying below its tangents.

Concave upward

f″(x)>0

Graph bends upward.

Concave downward

f″(x)<0

Graph bends downward.

Inflection pointA point P on y=f(x) is an inflection point if f is continuous there and the concavity changes from upward to downward or downward to upward.
Second Derivative TestSuppose f′(c)=0 and f″ is continuous near c. If f″(c)>0, f has a local minimum at c. If f″(c)<0, f has a local maximum at c. If f″(c)=0, the test is inconclusive.
Method
  1. Compute f″(x).
  2. Find where f″=0 or undefined, plus domain breaks.
  3. Make a sign chart for f″.
  4. State concavity on each interval.
  5. Call a point an inflection point only if the function is continuous there and concavity actually changes.
Worked example

For f(x)=x³−3x, f′=3x²−3 and f″=6x.

f″<0 for x<0: concave downward.
f″>0 for x>0: concave upward.
Concavity changes at x=0 and f is continuous there. The inflection point is (0,0).
For stationary points, f′=0 at x=−1,1. f″(−1)=−6 gives a local maximum; f″(1)=6 gives a local minimum.

Indeterminate forms and L’Hôpital’s Rule

Lecture 01 · pp. 28–32Reference · Side A §9

Forms covered in the lecture

FormWhat to do
0/0 or ∞/∞Check L’Hôpital’s conditions; differentiate numerator and denominator separately.
0·∞Rewrite the product as a quotient, then aim for 0/0 or ∞/∞.
∞−∞Combine terms into one quotient or rationalize if possible.
0⁰, ∞⁰, 1∞Set y=f(x)g(x), take ln y=g ln f, evaluate the transformed limit, then exponentiate.

The lecture explicitly notes that 0∞ is not an indeterminate form.

L’Hôpital’s RuleIf f and g are differentiable on an open interval containing a (possibly except at a), g′(x)≠0 there, and the original ratio has form 0/0 or ∞/∞, then lim f/g = lim f′/g′ if the latter limit exists (including ±∞).

The rule also applies to one-sided limits and limits as x→∞ or x→−∞. Re-check the form after every application; repeat only if the new quotient remains indeterminate and the conditions hold.

Method · choose the correct transformation
  1. Evaluate numerator and denominator limits separately.
  2. Use L’Hôpital only for 0/0 or ∞/∞.
  3. For 0·∞, rewrite as f/(1/g) or g/(1/f).
  4. For ∞−∞, combine into a single quotient or rationalize.
  5. For an indeterminate power, take logarithms, evaluate the exponent limit, then use e to recover the original limit.
Worked example · 0/0

Evaluate limx→1 ln x/(x−1).

Both numerator and denominator tend to 0, so the form is 0/0.
Differentiate top and bottom: (1/x)/1.
Take the limit: 1.
Worked example · 0·∞

Evaluate limx→0⁺ x ln x.

x→0 and ln x→−∞, so the product is indeterminate.
Rewrite as ln x/(1/x), which is −∞/∞? More directly, use (ln x)/(1/x), then apply L’Hôpital.
The derivative ratio is (1/x)/(−1/x²)=−x, which tends to 0.
The limit is 0.
Worked example · ∞/∞ with repeated L’Hôpital

For limx→∞eˣ/x², the first derivative ratio is eˣ/(2x), still ∞/∞. Apply the rule again: eˣ/2→∞. Therefore the original limit is ∞.

Worked example · ∞−∞

Evaluate limx→∞(√(x²+x)−x).

The expression is ∞−∞. Multiply and divide by the conjugate.
[(√(x²+x)−x)(√(x²+x)+x)]/[√(x²+x)+x] = x/[√(x²+x)+x].
Divide top and bottom by x: 1/[√(1+1/x)+1] → 1/2.
Worked example · indeterminate power

Evaluate limx→0⁺xx, the lecture’s power-form example.

Let y=xx. Then ln y=x ln x.
Rewrite x ln x as ln x/(1/x). L’Hôpital gives (1/x)/(−1/x²)=−x→0.
Thus ln y→0 and y→e⁰. The limit is 1.

Worked A–H example · y=2x²/(x²−1)

A · Domain

x²−1≠0, so x≠−1,1.

B · Intercepts

Numerator is zero at x=0; y-intercept is also 0. Intercept: (0,0).

C · Symmetry

f(−x)=f(x), so the function is even and symmetric about the y-axis.

D · Asymptotes

Vertical: x=−1 and x=1. Horizontal: as x→±∞, f(x)→2, so y=2.

E · Increase / decrease

f′(x)=−4x/(x²−1)². It is positive for x<0 and negative for x>0, with domain breaks at ±1. Increasing on (−∞,−1) and (−1,0); decreasing on (0,1) and (1,∞).

F · Local extrema

At x=0, f′ changes + to −, so (0,0) is a local maximum.

G · Concavity / inflection

f″(x)=4(3x²+1)/(x²−1)³. Concave up on (−∞,−1) and (1,∞); concave down on (−1,1). There is no inflection point at ±1 because the function is not defined there.

Draw two vertical asymptotes at x=±1 and horizontal asymptote y=2. Use even symmetry, the maximum at the origin, the sign of f′ and the concavity intervals to shape the three branches.

20 · Integrals, applications and techniques · 2.1

The area problem and rectangle approximations

Lecture 02 · pp. 1–5Reference · Side B §11–12, §17

The area problem asks for the area under a continuous curve y=f(x), from x=a to x=b, when f(x)≥0. A curved region is approximated using vertical rectangles; increasing the number of strips improves the approximation.

Equal-width subdivision

Δx=b−anxi=a+iΔx

Here n is the number of strips; Δx is the width of each strip; x0=a and xn=b. A rectangle’s area is width × height.

Right-endpoint sum

Rn=∑i=1nf(xi)Δx

Left-endpoint sum

Ln=∑i=1nf(xi−1)Δx

For a continuous nonnegative function, the area is the common limit of the left- and right-endpoint sums as n→∞. For a nonnegative increasing function, left rectangles underestimate and right rectangles overestimate. For a nonnegative decreasing function, the reverse is true.

Worked example · f(x)=x² on [0,1], n=4
Δx=1/4. Right endpoints: 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, 1.
R₄=(1/4)[(1/4)²+(1/2)²+(3/4)²+1²]=0.46875.
Left endpoints: 0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4. L₄=(1/4)[0²+(1/4)²+(1/2)²+(3/4)²]=0.21875.
Thus 0.21875 < A < 0.46875.
Worked example · exact area as a limit

For f(x)=x² on [0,1], using right endpoints:

Δx=1/n and xᵢ=i/n. Therefore Rₙ=(1/n)Σ(i/n)²=(1/n³)Σi².
Use Σi²=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6: Rₙ=(n+1)(2n+1)/(6n²).
Taking n→∞ gives A=lim Rₙ=1/3. The left-endpoint sum has the same limit.

The lecture also lists other uses of definite-integral limits: curve lengths, volumes, centers of mass, force due to water pressure, work and related quantities. It does not develop formulas for those applications in this section.

The definite integral: definition, notation and net area

Lecture 02 · pp. 6–8Reference · Side B §11

Definition

If f is defined on [a,b], divide the interval into n equal subintervals of width Δx=(b−a)/n. Choose a sample point xᵢ* in each subinterval. If the limit exists and is the same for all possible choices of sample points, f is integrable and the limit is the definite integral.

∫abf(x)dx=limn→∞∑i=1nf(xi*)Δx

Parts of the notation

  • ∫ is the integral sign.
  • f(x) is the integrand.
  • a is the lower limit; b is the upper limit.
  • dx identifies the variable of integration.
  • x is a dummy variable: ∫f(x)dx = ∫f(t)dt = ∫f(r)dr over the same limits.
  • A definite integral is a number; it does not depend on the dummy variable.
Integrability theorem in the lectureIf f is continuous on [a,b], or has only a finite number of jump discontinuities, then f is integrable on [a,b].

Area and signed area

If f≥0, the definite integral is the area under the curve. If f takes both positive and negative values, the integral is the net area: area above the x-axis minus area below it.

∫abf(x)dx=A+−A−

Geometric area counts all regions positively. If the graph crosses the x-axis, split the interval at its roots and add the positive magnitudes; equivalently use ∫|f(x)|dx.

FTC Part 1: the accumulation function

Lecture 02 · pp. 14–17Reference · Side B §18

Let f be continuous on [a,b]. Define the accumulation function:

G(x)=∫axf(t)dt

For a positive f, G(x) is the area accumulated from a to x—the “area so far”. The variable t is the dummy variable; x is the moving upper limit.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus · Part 1If f is continuous on [a,b], then G is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and G′(x)=f(x). Also G(a)=0.

Because G′(x)=f(x), G increases where f(x)>0 and decreases where f(x)<0. If f changes from positive to negative, G changes from increasing to decreasing and has a local maximum there.

Worked example · lecture p. 17

Find the derivative of G(x)=∫₀ˣ(1+sin t)dt.

The integrand 1+sin t is continuous.
Apply FTC1: G′(x)=1+sin x.

FTC Part 2: evaluate a definite integral using an antiderivative

Lecture 02 · pp. 17–18Reference · Side B §§19–20

An antiderivative F of f is a function whose derivative is f: F′=f. If f is continuous on [a,b], FTC2 evaluates the definite integral by subtracting the endpoint values of an antiderivative.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus · Part 2∫abf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)where F′=f.

Notation: F(x)|ab = F(b)−F(a) = [F(x)]ab.

Method
  1. Find an antiderivative F of the integrand.
  2. Evaluate F at the upper limit b.
  3. Evaluate F at the lower limit a.
  4. Subtract: F(b)−F(a).

Differentiation and integration as inverse processes

Lecture 02 · p. 18Reference · Side B §§18–21

The two parts of the Fundamental Theorem connect differentiation and integration as inverse processes.

FTC1 · differentiate an accumulation function

ddx∫axf(t)dt=f(x)

FTC2 · evaluate using an antiderivative

∫abF′(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)

Checking an antiderivative

  • Differentiate the proposed F(x).
  • If F′(x)=f(x), it is an antiderivative of f.
  • For a definite integral, any additive constant cancels in F(b)−F(a).
Worked check

Claim: F(x)=x³/3 is an antiderivative of f(x)=x².

Differentiate: F′(x)=x².
This matches f(x), so the claim is correct.
